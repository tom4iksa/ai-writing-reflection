    Station 1 · Arrival

    Jumping off points: what AI disruption can teach a writer

    A reflection on the AIM Lab Lecture Series talk by Dr. Sam Meekings, “Jumping Off Points: Making Opportunities from AI Disruptions in the Writing Classroom,” Northwestern University in Qatar.

    Hi, I'm Tomiris. Walk the map with me. Every station unlocks the next one.

    The argument

    Generative AI disrupts writing, and students already use it.

    Meekings argues that teachers should therefore demonstrate, discuss and dissect AI in class instead of banning it.

    His 2026 article makes the same case: process, authorship and originality need new definitions (Meekings, 2026).

    My position

    I agree with this method, because it is how a journalist treats any powerful source.

    I add one condition: studying AI in class must also mean studying how it was built and whose work it used.

    The core reading

    Meekings' article “AI Won't Eat Your Brain” is the written version of the lecture's argument (Meekings, 2026).

    It redefines three concepts.

    Process becomes the centre of teaching and assessment, with prompt logs, messy drafts and reflexive writing valued over the final product.

    Authorship is widened: Meekings borrows Sharples' description of the writer as a designer of text and imagines the author as the manager of a creative team.

    Originality shifts towards craft and diversity, because AI reproduces Western defaults such as the three-act structure and conflict as the engine of every story.

    His answer is to bring in other traditions, such as the nested tales of the 1001 Nights or the four-part Japanese kishōtenketsu.

    His wider work, in four drops

    Wonder and Loss (2025)Four parts: writing as discovery, transformation, renewal and magic. Writing is a path through experience, which is why handing it to a machine costs something.
    Barriers to creative writing in Qatar (2023)Interviews with students point to socio-cultural barriers and internal conflicts. The classroom he describes is our classroom.
    Digital literacy in the classroom (2021)Social media as a lab for voice and world-building, with its problems named. AI is the next platform for the same method.
    The workshop in the 21st century (2026)A co-edited critique of the Iowa workshop model. Feedback has always been collaborative; AI becomes one more reader.

    Station 6 · My Reflection

    Where I agree and where I push back

    What the talk means for me as a journalism student who writes about AI startups.

    This is the part I wrote for myself.

    Where I agree

    The strongest idea in the talk is the shift from product to process.

    In journalism, the published story is the smallest part of the work; its value lies in the calls, documents and interviews behind it.

    Meekings' distinction between translating and generating names this clearly.

    I also agree that diversity matters more than originality.

    AI drafts drift towards one familiar Western shape, while the Kazakh aitys, an improvised poetic duel, follows a call-and-response logic no default prompt suggests.

    My own traditions are a resource that AI does not have.

    Where I push back

    AI is permanent, but the terms of its use are still being decided in courts, libraries and writers' unions.

    A judge approved a $1.5 billion settlement over pirated books while ruling that training on lawfully acquired books was legal (Associated Press, 2026).

    The Society of Authors helped launch an ethical AI charter and left it within days after its members objected (Brown, 2026).

    As Dean Marwan Kraidy said at NU-Q's Rawabet conference, “Nothing is inevitable” (Northwestern University in Qatar, 2025c).

    Teaching AI therefore means teaching students to question these terms, not only to use the tools.

    AI as a critic helps only a student who can still judge the critique, a risk Meekings has discussed with Spencer Striker as “cognitive debt” (Northwestern University in Qatar, n.d.-b).

    Prompt logs prove authorship better than detectors, but only when students can declare AI use without penalty (Meekings, 2026, p. 11).

    AI can read my draft. It does not write it.My takeaway

    What this means for me as a journalist

    The table at Station 3 has no row for accountability, and journalism is built on that row.

    In my coverage of AI startups for Forbes Kazakhstan, I treat AI output as a source: fast, useful, unverified and biased.

    A source can inform a story, but it cannot take responsibility for it.

    The reporting and the writing stay mine.
